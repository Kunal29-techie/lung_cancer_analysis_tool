Overview

Lung cancer diagnosis typically begins with a series of tests to confirm the presence of cancer and determine its type and stage. Early detection is crucial for effective treatment, so it's important to be proactive if you are at risk or experiencing symptoms.

1. Diagnostic Tests

To diagnose lung cancer, doctors use several tests, which may include the following:

  • Chest X-ray - Often the first step in detecting lung cancer, an X-ray can reveal abnormalities in the lungs, such as tumors.
  • CT Scan (Computed Tomography) - A CT scan provides more detailed images of the lungs and can help identify the size, shape, and location of a tumor.
  • Bronchoscopy - This procedure involves using a thin tube with a camera to examine the airways and take samples from abnormal areas of the lung.
  • Biopsy - A sample of tissue is removed from the tumor and examined under a microscope to determine if it is cancerous.
  • PET Scan (Positron Emission Tomography) - A PET scan can help detect whether the cancer has spread to other parts of the body.

Doctors will choose the most appropriate tests based on the patient’s symptoms, risk factors, and initial examination.

2. Staging

Once lung cancer is diagnosed, determining its stage is important to plan the most effective treatment. Staging describes the extent of the cancer and whether it has spread:

  • Stage 0 (Carcinoma in Situ) - The cancer is confined to the surface layer of the lung and has not spread.
  • Stage I - The cancer is localized to the lung and has not spread to the lymph nodes or other parts of the body.
  • Stage II - The cancer may have spread to nearby lymph nodes but is still confined to the chest.
  • Stage III - The cancer has spread to lymph nodes or other tissues within the chest.
  • Stage IV - The cancer has spread to distant organs such as the liver, bones, or brain.

Staging helps doctors determine the best treatment options and predict the prognosis. Staging can be done using imaging tests, biopsies, and other procedures.
